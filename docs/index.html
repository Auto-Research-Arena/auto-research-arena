1. The Benchmark Shapes the Research

AI agents are moving beyond helping researchers write and reason toward carrying out parts of the research process. In auto-research, a coding-capable agent repeatedly proposes a change, implements it, runs an experiment, and uses the result to decide what to try next. An auto-research harness organizes this loop: it determines how ideas are proposed, which results are retained, and how the search continues.

Progress in auto-research depends on a good benchmark. First, a benchmark gives every harness the same starting program and evaluator, providing a common basis for comparing methods. More importantly, it defines how research progresses: after every experiment, the benchmark score determines which proposal survives, what code the next iteration starts from, and which line of inquiry can continue. Unlike conventional benchmarks, which typically evaluate a finished system, an auto-research benchmark operates throughout the research process. A benchmark does not merely measure research; it shapes the research an agent performs.

Existing benchmarks show this effect. Karpathy’s Auto-Research minimizes loss under fixed training time; NanoGPT-Bench minimizes training time under fixed loss. In both settings, agents improve the benchmark score primarily through hyperparameter tuning and local modifications, while broader algorithmic discoveries remain comparatively rare1. Their resulting idea frontier closely reflects what each benchmark rewards: a method that preserves loss while reducing parameters, memory, data, or inference cost cannot advance under a loss-only objective.

This matters because research capability is not defined by any single target. Individual research projects often optimize one target, but research as a whole advances across many partly independent targets. Different directions proceed in parallel, and their advances accumulate: a method developed for one target can become the foundation for progress on another. In language modeling, the Transformer improved training parallelism, Multi-Query Attention reduced inference memory and decoding cost, and FlashAttention reduced memory traffic. Together, these parallel and sequential advances—not progress along any single metric—form the history of the field.

This view directly motivates Auto-Research Arena. Arena separates what makes a result valid from what makes it better. Validation loss and relevant resource limits determine whether a result is admissible; a selected target metric determines progress among admissible results. This separation allows the research target to vary without relaxing a common standard of model quality. We instantiate this formulation in language modeling with nine objectives spanning model size, data use, training, and inference (Figure 1 and Table 1).

This formulation serves research and evaluation in complementary ways. As a research environment, different targets create distinct selection pressures, allowing different kinds of ideas to be discovered and retained. As a benchmark, the nine objective tracks provide a more comprehensive measure of auto-research ability: they test whether a harness can make valid progress across different research problems, rather than excel on a single objective.

Arena supports a broad range of model and systems ideas. The selected programs include looped Transformers, grouped-query attention, shared token representations, selective activation recomputation and fused inference kernels. Different efficiency objectives make different mechanisms useful (Section 3). Additional experiments examine how ideas can carry across objectives (Section 5).

Arena evaluates research methods across diverse objectives. The selected programs reveal complementary strengths in model size, training, data use and inference. No method achieves the lowest value on every objective. The benchmark defines quality and resource constraints for each task (Section 4).

Arena also supports systematic studies of auto-research. By varying targets, constraints, research budgets, and starting histories, we study how the formulation and organization of a research problem change both measurable progress and the ideas that emerge (Section 5).

Accordingly, this work makes three contributions:

  • A broader idea frontier. Arena reformulates progress as multi-metric constrained optimization, creating selection pressure for different model and systems advances. Its selected programs span recurrent Transformers, attention redesign, training-memory reduction and inference optimization.
  • A more comprehensive benchmark. Nine complementary tracks evaluate seven harnesses by their admissible endpoint scores. Each objective measures efficiency under explicit quality and resource limits.
  • Studying the research process. Arena exposes how targets, constraints, budgets, and prior discoveries shape auto-research, with traces serving as explanatory evidence rather than a subjective ranking criterion.

2. Auto-Research as Constrained Optimization

For each efficiency target, Arena defines a constrained optimization problem. The target is the resource cost to minimize; task quality and the remaining resource costs are constraints. Let p denote a candidate program:

minimize target(p) Selected target
subject to
quality(p) is acceptableQuality requirement
costj(p)≤limitjOther resource limits

Candidates must also modify only permitted files and pass correctness checks.

Efficiency targets

Efficiency targets capture the resources required to build and deploy models: parameters and data for model development, computation, memory, and time during training, and memory and latency during inference. These targets are general; a benchmark instantiation specifies how each is measured and which quality and resource constraints apply.

A language-modeling instantiation

We instantiate these targets on Karpathy’s Auto-Research language-model testbed, where agents edit an LLM pretraining program. Arena defines nine tracks spanning model, data, training, and inference efficiency. Model quality is measured by validation bits per byte (BPB) and must remain below 1.05, slightly above the starting model’s BPB to leave room for structural changes without materially degrading quality. Table 1 gives the exact metric and constraints for each track; Section 5 studies the effect of the quality threshold.

3. A Broader Idea Frontier for Auto-Research

Arena changes not only how auto-research is evaluated, but what it can discover. To examine the resulting idea frontier, we ran our auto-research harness across Arena’s nine targets. The searches produced a broad set of promising model and systems ideas, many closely connected to active research.

From loss-driven search to a broader idea frontier

Karpathy’s Auto-Research optimizes validation loss. Beyond hyperparameter tuning, its reported search [2] discovered two architectural changes: smear, which mixes information from the previous token into the current representation, and backout, which modifies the model’s final residual readout. Both discoveries serve the same goal: improving model quality (Table 2).

4. Benchmarking Auto-Research Harnesses

We compare seven existing search methods across the same nine efficiency objectives. The comparison includes 63 selected programs. Their scores determine the aggregate ranking; program and trace analysis explain how the solutions differ.

Harnesses and Evaluation

Our two reference harnesses isolate simple search strategies: Sequential Search follows a single line of research, while Beam Search retains several promising programs. AutoScientists coordinates teams of agents; Arbor organizes hypotheses into a tree; GEAR evolves a population of research states; and Heuresis MAP-Elites preserves strong candidates with different characteristics. TPE serves as a non-agent baseline restricted to a predefined set of model and training hyperparameters (Table 4).

Table 4. Auto-research harnesses Search strategies, implementation details, and references
How the seven harnesses organize the research loop
HarnessWhat it does
Sequential SearchFollow one line of research. Each valid improvement becomes the starting program for the next experiment; rejected changes are not kept as branches.
Beam SearchKeep several promising programs. The next experiment can build on any retained program, so a branch can survive even when it is not yet the best.
AutoScientists [41]Use multiple agents that read a shared experiment history, form teams around different hypotheses, and critique proposals before running them. Successes and failures are shared so teams can redirect their search.
Arbor [42]Use a long-lived coordinator to maintain a tree of hypotheses and evidence. Short-lived executors test individual branches; the coordinator then expands, prunes, or combines them.
GEAR [43]Keep a bounded population of research states. A fixed controller selects parents using past productivity, novelty, and coverage, then proposes either a mutation or a combination of ideas from two parents.
Heuresis MAP-Elites [45]Preserve resource-diverse candidates in a MAP-Elites archive, then propose mutations or crossovers from its strongest candidates.
TPE [44]Use a Tree-structured Parzen Estimator to model which settings tend to perform well and choose the next settings to test. Unlike the six LLM-based harnesses, it searches a predefined hyperparameter space and cannot invent new code structures.

Constraints Make the Scores Meaningful

A lower target value counts as progress only when the evaluator verifies every quality and resource constraint. An invalid candidate cannot count as a valid benchmark result, regardless of how attractive its target value appears.

TPE measured a 221.18 ms decoding candidate, faster than its 230.74 ms valid endpoint, but its validation BPB was 1.062 rather than below 1.05. AutoScientist reached the quality threshold after 182.98 million tokens, better than its 184.549M valid endpoint, but the program changed Arena’s fixed training-clock protocol. Neither candidate changes the corresponding benchmark score.

Explaining Scores Through Search Behavior

Similar endpoint scores can conceal different research capabilities. On total parameters, five harnesses finish between 13.20 million and 15.15 million parameters, yet their final programs rely on different mechanisms: GEAR combines recurrent Transformer blocks with shared input and value embeddings, Beam Search combines multi-query attention with shared value embeddings, and Sequential Search reuses Transformer blocks across depth.

The clearest difference is not which ideas are proposed, but which ideas survive the developing program. Beam Search and Heuresis both tested input–output embedding tying. Beam Search’s tested branch reached 1.0558 BPB and failed the quality gate. Heuresis retained a vocabulary tensor shared across input, value embeddings, and output. The same mechanism can therefore succeed or fail in different developing programs.

Benchmarking conclusion

Our simple Sequential Search and Beam Search harnesses remain highly competitive with more elaborate systems, with Sequential Search ranking second in the comparison. These results suggest that existing auto-research harnesses still leave substantial room for improvement. We hypothesize that some designs have overfit to earlier, less comprehensive benchmarks, where success on a narrow objective may not translate into broader research capability. For example, the TPE implementation evaluated here comes from a system developed around Karpathy’s auto-research and searches a predefined hyperparameter space, leaving many architectural and systems changes outside its reach. This limitation illustrates the risk of benchmark-specific specialization, although it does not by itself establish overfitting. By evaluating multiple efficiency objectives and examining the ideas explored, Arena provides a more comprehensive assessment of harness capabilities and limitations, helping guide harness design toward methods that generalize across research objectives.

5. The Science of Auto-Research

Arena also supports studies of the research process. The budget, quality-threshold and cross-objective examples in this section come from additional experiments conducted separately from the main benchmark comparison. They examine how research settings and starting histories relate to the ideas and outcomes observed.

We study four aspects of this process: how research budget is allocated, how quality constraints shape exploration, how discoveries carry across objectives, and how routine optimization and novel mechanisms interact. The observed traces show that longer experiments are not always better, quality slack can broaden exploration, gains can accumulate with trade-offs, and routine optimization can prepare viable parents for novel mechanisms.

Research Budget Allocation

A research budget can support longer tests of individual ideas or more rounds of proposal and revision. Extra training may help a modified model recover quality; additional rounds give the agent more opportunities to test alternatives and build on earlier results.

We study this distinction in a separate total-parameter sweep with 1,200, 2,400, 4,800, or 6,000 seconds per candidate and a total training budget of 20 GPU-hours per search. Alongside the smallest eligible model sizes, we examine completed experiments, generations, and the resulting technical ideas. Figure 2 shows that the endpoints neither improve nor worsen monotonically with longer candidate runs.

The shortest-duration setting completed more candidates and found the most compact eligible model through private and shared token representations. The longer-duration settings used recurrence, weight tying, or width reduction, but all found larger eligible models. The difference is therefore visible in both the results and the research paths.

These observations motivate studying how compute is allocated, not just how much is available. At this fixed budget, longer candidate runs allow fewer experiments, so this sweep does not isolate their individual effects or establish a scaling law.

Takeaway · Research budget

In this sweep, longer candidate runs do not necessarily yield better results; budget allocation is part of the research strategy.

Quality Constraints and Exploration

In Section 2’s formulation, the agent minimizes a resource cost subject to a quality threshold. Here we vary that constraint to ask how it changes the ideas agents explore.

We compare BPB thresholds of 1.02, 1.05, and 2.0 while keeping the starting program, data, seed, hardware, evaluator, and 600-second training budget per candidate fixed. Each search contributes its first 30 completed candidate evaluations. The traces show a change in search behavior. At 1.02, early experiments often adjust the batch and learning schedule to earn quality margin. At 1.05, searches move directly into cuts to value tables, MLPs, depth, and attention. At 2.0, they retain much more compressed, lower-quality models and explore recurrence and shared token representations.

Figure 3 shows the corresponding smallest eligible model sizes of 23.6M, 19.7M, and 465K for Beam Search, and 26.5M, 18.9M, and 692K for Sequential Search. Peak training memory similarly falls from thousands of MiB at 1.02 to 166.4 MiB for Beam Search and 35.6 MiB for Sequential Search at 2.0. These are outcomes under different quality requirements, not equivalent-quality improvements.

The research implication goes beyond smaller models: quality slack can give structural ideas room to develop before they would satisfy a stricter requirement. Other search settings and total wall-clock time differ across these experiments, so the comparison suggests a change in exploration rather than isolating the threshold’s causal effect.

Takeaway · Quality constraints

In these traces, looser quality constraints give structural ideas more room to develop.

Cumulative Research Across Objectives

We examine whether switching the optimization target can produce new gains while preserving earlier ones.

To study this, we initialize a new search from a program optimized for another objective. We measure improvement on the new target and, where the receiving evaluator supports it, check how much of the earlier benefit remains. Figure 4 shows both accumulation and trade-offs.

In a parameters-to-FLOPs search, training FLOPs fell from 113.80M to 78.42M per token while the winning path reduced the starting program’s measured parameter count from 14.09M to 13.90M. The new improvement was compatible with the earlier one.

Takeaway · Cumulative research

These searches show that research can build across objectives, but new gains may trade away part of an earlier improvement.

How Novel Ideas Develop During Search

Novel ideas often develop on top of routine optimization. Across the search traces, familiar changes to width, depth, batch size, and learning rate frequently establish a viable parent—a program that satisfies the quality and resource constraints. The agent can then explore riskier structural changes that alter how the model represents information or performs computation. This is a recurring pattern in the observed lineages, rather than a required order for every run.

A GEAR parameter-count trace illustrates this progression. Routine adjustments to width and batch size first reduced parameter count from 37.75M to 23.59M. After sharing weights across all four layer pairs failed the quality requirement, the harness shared weights across three layer pairs and reused the input embedding table for value embeddings. These structural changes reduced parameter count further to 15.14M—an additional 35.8% reduction.

Takeaway · Novelty and tuning

Routine optimization creates viable foundations; novel mechanisms extend them, but still require correctness and utility checks.

Endpoint scores are sufficient for ranking harnesses; understanding auto-research as a scientific process requires a different lens. Arena makes resource allocation, quality requirements, research history, and idea development observable, allowing future benchmark instantiations to test whether the same relationships hold in other research domains.

6. Bring Your Own Auto-Research Harness to Arena

Auto-Research Arena evaluates harnesses without prescribing how they search. To bring a new auto-research harness to Arena, it needs only to inspect the provided workspace, modify the allowed program, launch experiments, read standardized results, and decide what to try next. Get started with the Auto-Research Arena GitHub repository.

Every run produces a terminal benchmark score together with a standardized research record: the initial program, proposed ideas, candidate implementations, execution outcomes, measured metrics, admissibility decisions, and the sequence of changes leading to the final program. The score determines the benchmark result. The accompanying record makes that result auditable and supports post-hoc analysis of how the harness searched.

The intended workflow is deliberately lightweight: keep the auto-research harness intact, replace its experimental environment with Auto-Research Arena, and select an objective. A harness may use a single incumbent, a beam, a population, a hypothesis tree, a multi-agent discussion, or another search structure. Arena defines the research problem, but not the researcher: it fixes the experimental world while leaving each harness free to organize exploration.

Single-metric benchmarks were an important first step because they made auto-research legible. The next step is not to discard that clarity, but to repeat the experiment across objectives—and, ultimately, across research domains. Different metrics expose different bottlenecks. Parallel searches expand the research frontier; discoveries carried across objectives turn that frontier into a research history.

Arena separates what makes a result admissible from what makes it better. This turns different resource targets into distinct research problems, makes their outcomes directly scoreable, and provides a shared setting in which their discoveries can accumulate into a research history.

Limitations

The evidence comes from one instantiation of Arena in language modeling. These results should be read as benchmark observations and harness-generated hypotheses; they do not establish that the same search behavior or discoveries will appear in other research domains, nor do they establish general causal rankings among auto-research harnesses. Several comparisons use one run per harness; searches complete different numbers of experiments and stop for different reasons; some experiments use different harness versions; and the analysis does not include a matched ablation suite or hidden final evaluation. Latency, time-to-target, and other system measurements also require repeated evaluation before small differences can support causal claims.

Novelty is another source of uncertainty. We group closely related edits into idea families and distinguish routine tuning from mechanisms that change how the program represents, computes, or retains information. This taxonomy helps organize the traces, but it is not an objective measure of scientific novelty; readers should treat the classifications as interpretations of the evidence rather than definitive novelty judgments.
